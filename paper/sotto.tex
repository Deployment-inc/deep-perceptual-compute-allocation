\begin{abstract}
Neural text-to-speech (TTS) systems spend roughly the same inference effort on every part of an utterance, even though human hearing is far more sensitive to some sounds than to others. Audio codecs have exploited this asymmetry for decades by spending \emph{bits} only where listeners can notice them. We propose \textbf{perceptual compute allocation}: spending synthesis \emph{compute} according to audibility rather than uniformly, a direction that matters most on CPU, where saved operations convert directly into latency and serving capacity. This paper presents the idea together with preliminary evidence from two CPU TTS families (VITS/HiFi-GAN and StyleTTS2/iSTFTNet), not a finished system. We find that a large fraction of time--frequency output can be removed with far less perceptual degradation when chosen by audibility than at random; that a trivial signal-energy criterion matches or beats three psychoacoustic masking-model implementations at locating removable content, so no heavyweight listener model is needed; and, in an informal single-listener blind ABX pilot, that deletion of up to 60--75\% of bins was not reliably distinguishable from the original, which motivates but does not establish a transparency claim. We also report what does \emph{not} work: dynamic INT8 quantization of the dominant decoder is up to $8\times$ slower on CPU, so post-hoc precision reduction cannot realize the savings. We argue that the realization is architectural (decoders that decline to synthesize what listeners cannot hear) and outline what such decoders would require. We release tooling, corpus, listening-study instrument, and the complete harness, including negative results.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\pagestyle{plain}

Consider the utterance ``Sure, your order 4-8-2 ships Tuesday.'' It contains pauses, steady vowel interiors whose loud harmonics mask inter-harmonic error, fricatives whose fine structure the ear does not track, and plosive bursts and digits where the listener is maximally sensitive. A neural TTS system spends identical compute on every one of these regions. The waste is invisible on GPU, where batch parallelism hides per-item savings; it is visible on CPU, where cost-sensitive voice serving lives and every skipped operation returns directly as latency.

The human auditory system provides a principled map of where fidelity matters. A psychoacoustic model (PAM) of the kind inside MPEG-1 Layer III~\cite{iso11172} computes, for any frame of audio, a per-band \emph{masking threshold} below which added error is inaudible~\cite{painter2000perceptual, zwicker2007psychoacoustics}. This model has been used to allocate bits at encode time~\cite{painter2000perceptual}, to weight gradients at training time~\cite{zhen2018psychoacoustically, zhen2020calibration}, and to place adversarial perturbations. It has not, to our knowledge, been used to allocate inference computation during synthesis. The visual analogue is foveated rendering~\cite{guenter2012foveated, patney2016foveated} and, more recently, foveated neural reconstruction~\cite{kaplanyan2019deepfovea}: the PAM would be our eye-tracker.

We set out with three expectations. \emph{First}, that neural TTS produces a significant amount of perceptually redundant output. \emph{Second}, that a psychoacoustic model would be needed to find it. \emph{Third}, that identifying this redundancy would give a straightforward path to CPU savings. We fixed hypotheses and decision rules for all three before the main experiments (Section~\ref{sec:hyp}). Only the first expectation survived.

\textbf{Surprise 1: the removable pool is large.} Ordering time--frequency content by audibility and removing the least audible fraction leaves a reference-based perceptual metric nearly untouched at budgets where random removal of the same amount is destroyed (Table~\ref{tab:teaser}). An informal single-listener blind ABX pilot found removal of up to 60--75\% of bins not reliably distinguishable from the original (Section~\ref{sec:abx}); this is preliminary and a larger study is needed to turn it into a quantitative claim.

\textbf{Surprise 2: energy beats psychoacoustics.} The masking model is not needed to locate the pool. A trivial signal-energy ranking matches or beats three masking-model implementations at every matched budget, under two degradation operators, on both model families (Section~\ref{sec:h3}). For time--frequency removal, energy is a sufficient statistic of audibility.

\textbf{Surprise 3: knowing what is removable does not let existing architectures skip the work.} A fixed neural circuit does not get cheaper because its output is ignorable, and the obvious post-hoc precision mechanism, dynamic INT8 quantization of the dominant decoder, runs $8\times$ slower than full precision on AVX-VNNI Intel and $4.8\times$ slower on Apple Silicon (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}). The binding constraint is architectural, not perceptual.

We think the two failed expectations are as useful as the one that held. The second removes a piece of the original mechanism story and makes deployment cheaper: the ``eye-tracker'' costs nothing. The third names the actual open problem: allocation-native decoders that can decline to synthesize what listeners cannot hear.

\subsection{Contributions}

\begin{enumerate}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{Measurement of the perceptually removable pool} in two CPU TTS families, showing with a reference-based metric that audibility-ordered removal vastly outperforms random removal, with a single-listener blind ABX pilot as preliminary human corroboration (Sections~\ref{sec:h1}, \ref{sec:abx}).
    \item \textbf{A pre-registered criterion test with a negative result:} signal energy matches or beats a psychoacoustic masking model for deciding \emph{where} to remove content. The sophisticated listener model is not needed for this decision (Section~\ref{sec:h3}).
    \item \textbf{A characterization of the theoretical-vs-realized savings gap}, including the reproducible finding that dynamic INT8 quantization of the dominant decoder \emph{increases} wall-clock cost on both Intel and Apple CPUs (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).
    \item \textbf{Methodological artifacts:} a $\Delta$-probe that calibrates any inference-time PAM against a reference metric and an external codec anchor; evidence that PESQ's severities, though not its orderings, collapse on masked-removal distortions; and two design traps (criterion/operator matching, self-masking) that silently bias perceptual-gating experiments (Sections~\ref{sec:probe}, \ref{sec:traps}).
    \item \textbf{Open release} of tooling, corpus, study instrument, and the full harness, including the negative-result experiments (\url{\repourl}).
\end{enumerate}

We present an idea with preliminary evidence. We claim the principle and its CPU-TTS instantiation as a promising direction. We do \emph{not} claim that a masking model beats energy, and we do not claim a realized end-to-end speedup; neither is supported by our data.

\section{Background: The Listener Model}

Masking is signal-dependent. A loud tonal component raises the detection threshold of quieter components at nearby frequencies, with the masking skirt spreading over the critical (Bark) bands of hearing~\cite{zwicker2007psychoacoustics}; for tens of milliseconds after a loud transient, quieter sounds are inaudible (forward masking); and an absolute threshold of hearing floors everything. A PAM identifies tonal and noise maskers per frame, spreads their influence across Bark bands, and combines them with the absolute threshold into a \emph{global masking threshold} $T(t,f)$~\cite{iso11172, bosi2003introduction}. We define \emph{headroom} as signal energy minus threshold per time--frequency cell; where headroom is negative, the listener is, at that instant, effectively deaf. Because thresholds change with content, level, and time, exploiting them at inference requires a model running \emph{during} synthesis rather than a static mask.

\section{Hypotheses and Pre-Registered Decision Rules}
\label{sec:hyp}

\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{H1 (existence).} A substantial fraction of CPU-TTS synthesis produces time--frequency output a listener cannot perceive.
    \item \textbf{H2 (exploitability).} Making computation conditional on audibility reduces end-to-end wall-clock at transparent quality.
    \item \textbf{H3 (criterion).} At matched compute budgets, masking-model-guided allocation outperforms energy-guided and random allocation on quality. \emph{Pre-registered fallback:} if H3 fails, the work is content-adaptive synthesis inference with a rigorous perceptual evaluation, and the negative is reported as a finding.
\end{itemize}

\emph{Transparency} is defined relative to the full-compute output of the same model, text, and seed. \emph{Matched budgets} compare allocation policies at equal expected compute. The claim-discipline rules we followed are stated in Appendix~\ref{app:discipline}. After H3 failed at proxy level, we logged an amendment (before any further analysis) moving the primary claim to the principle and its measurement; no hypothesis was weakened to fit a result.

\section{Testbed and Method}

\subsection{Models, hardware, corpus}

We use two CPU-practical TTS families under ONNX Runtime~\cite{onnxruntime} with the CPU execution provider: \textbf{Piper}~\cite{piper} (voice \texttt{en\_US-lessac-medium}; a VITS~\cite{kim2021vits} system with a HiFi-GAN~\cite{kong2020hifigan} decoder, 22.05\,kHz) and \textbf{Kokoro v1.0}~\cite{kokoro} (a StyleTTS2~\cite{li2023styletts2} system with an iSTFTNet~\cite{kaneko2022istftnet} decoder, 24\,kHz). Hardware: an Apple M5 Pro (aarch64) and, for cross-architecture replication, an Intel Xeon Platinum 8488C (Sapphire Rapids, AVX-VNNI, 8 vCPU, AWS \texttt{c7i.2xlarge}). The corpus is 90 fixed-seed utterances over three domains: conversational agent speech with digits and entities, long-form narration, and romanized Hindi--English (Hinglish) agent speech, 15 per domain.\footnote{Hinglish is synthesized through an English voice and grapheme-to-phoneme model, as production English-voice agents do; a native Hindi voice is future work.}

\subsection{Psychoacoustic tooling}

We implement two PAMs on a common 1024-sample Hann / 512-hop STFT grid: (i) a from-scratch model in the MPEG-1 Model-1 lineage~\cite{iso11172, bosi2003introduction} (tonal/noise masker identification, two-slope spreading, absolute threshold after Terhardt~\cite{terhardt1979pitch}, forward temporal masking), and (ii) a vetted spreading-matrix model with nonlinear superposition ported from a reference audio-coding implementation~\cite{schuller2020filter}. Both are calibrated with the $\Delta$-probe of Section~\ref{sec:probe}. Model (i) is used for the Stage-1 existence measurement; model (ii), calibrated, for the final criterion test; the Model-1 results are retained because the conclusion is invariant to the choice.

\subsection{Oracle degradation harness}
\label{sec:harness}

To test \emph{placement} independently of any mechanism, we degrade a budget-matched set of (frame, Bark-band) cells of the reference synthesis and compare policies at equal budgets, scoring against the same-seed full output with wideband PESQ~\cite{itu_p862, rix2001pesq} and STOI~\cite{taal2011stoi} at 16\,kHz. Round-trip fidelity of the analysis/synthesis path is 232\,dB SNR (PESQ 4.64, the metric ceiling). Two operators model two mechanism classes: \textbf{deletion} (zero a cell; models skipping synthesis) and \textbf{noise injection} (add fixed-level noise; models reduced-precision synthesis). Budgets are matched on FFT-bin count, a proxy for compute, rather than on cell count (Bark cells have unequal width). Policies:
\begin{itemize}[leftmargin=*]
    \item \textbf{masking}: rank cells by signal-minus-threshold (deletion) or by threshold (noise), most maskable first;
    \item \textbf{energy}: rank by band energy, quietest first (deletion) or loudest first (noise, the uniform-SNR heuristic);
    \item \textbf{random}: fixed-seed uniform ranking.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Two traps in perceptual-gating experiments}
\label{sec:traps}

Two design errors in our early iterations changed conclusions and are, we believe, general. \textbf{Criterion/operator matching:} ``signal is below threshold'' licenses \emph{deletion}; ``threshold is high enough to hide the error'' licenses \emph{noise}. Pairing a deletion criterion with a noise operator produced artifactual low-budget losses. \textbf{Self-masking:} a noise-like cell's threshold includes its own masker contribution, so it can justify its own removal: delete it and the masking that excused the deletion vanishes. Deletion decisions must use \emph{exclusive} (leave-self-out) thresholds. All results below use matched pairings and exclusive thresholds for deletion.

\subsection{H3: the criterion test}
\label{sec:h3}

\textbf{Audibility ordering is what matters.} Removing time--frequency content ordered by audibility is scored transparent by PESQ up to substantial budgets, whereas removing the same number of bins at random collapses PESQ toward 1.1--2.3 (Table~\ref{tab:h3}, Figure~\ref{fig:h3}). Allocating by audibility rather than uniformly is the entire game; the principle is strongly supported.

\textbf{But energy is a sufficient statistic.} The pre-registered comparison of masking-guided against energy-guided placement gives the same answer under every variant we tried: the from-scratch Model-1 PAM (inclusive and exclusive thresholds), and the vetted, PESQ-calibrated spreading-matrix PAM. Energy matches or beats masking at every matched budget, both operators, both models, with paired Wilcoxon signed-rank $p<10^{-6}$ throughout (45 utterances per model per condition for Model-1; 24 for the vetted model); masking never significantly wins. Under the noise operator the ordering is the same, with the gap shrinking toward parity at the lowest budgets (PESQ 4.63 vs 4.63 at $-45$\,dB noise-to-signal on Kokoro) and both policies beating uniform placement by 1.5--2 PESQ points.

\textbf{Interpretation.} For deciding \emph{where} a listener cannot hear removed content, signal energy already captures nearly all the usable information; the masking model's additional machinery (spreading, inter-band masking, absolute threshold) does not improve the decision. Under the pre-registered fallback, the paper stands on the principle, the measurement, and this negative.

\subsection{The $\Delta$-probe and a metric caveat}
\label{sec:probe}

An inference-time PAM must be trusted before its decisions are acted on. We introduce a \textbf{$\Delta$-probe}: inject noise shaped exactly at the PAM's own threshold curve, offset by $\Delta$\,dB, and locate the $\Delta$ at which a reference metric declares transparency. A correctly calibrated PAM reaches transparency near $\Delta=0$; the crossing measures its miscalibration. As an external anchor, LAME MP3 round-trips of our corpus audio at 128 and 64\,kbps score PESQ 4.64 and 4.54: the metric is content with noise at \emph{real} codec thresholds. Our from-scratch Model-1 implementation required $\Delta\approx-30$ to $-40$\,dB (its thresholds were tens of decibels too permissive, traced to band-summed noise maskers); the vetted spreading-matrix model required $\Delta\approx-16$\,dB under codec-realistic capping of the noise at the signal magnitude (Figure~\ref{fig:probe}), consistent with its lack of tonality handling on strongly harmonic speech. The probe is a reusable, one-number validation for any PAM used at inference.

\textbf{Metric caveat.} PESQ's \emph{orderings} were informative throughout, but its \emph{severities} are not to be trusted on this distortion class: in the listening pilot (Section~\ref{sec:abx}), clips a listener could not distinguish from the reference received PESQ scores as low as 1.2--3.5. PESQ was validated on telephony degradations, not masked spectral removal. We therefore use PESQ as an ordering-only screen and require human judgement for any transparency claim.

\subsection{RQ3: can inaudibility be predicted before the waveform exists?}
\label{sec:rq3}

Masking thresholds estimated from 80-band mel-resolution features (a proxy for pre-waveform representations) agree with ground-truth thresholds to 2.8\,dB RMSE and 96\% cell-decision agreement on both models, but the unsafe direction (predicted inaudible, actually audible) occurs in 11.4\% (Piper) and 12.5\% (Kokoro) of predicted-maskable cells (30 utterances). Predictive gating is feasible with a conservative margin; draft-based verification on the produced audio remains the safe fallback.

\subsection{RQ4: theoretical versus realized savings}
\label{sec:rq4}

Removing perceptually unnecessary output saves nothing until the computation is made conditional. We tested the most obvious post-hoc precision mechanism as an aligned instance of ``spend fewer bits where error is inaudible'': dynamic INT8 quantization of the dominant decoder, after splitting Kokoro's graph at its five encoder/decoder boundary tensors (the split is bit-exact against the monolithic model). Table~\ref{tab:int8} reports the result. The INT8 decoder is $8.1\times$ \emph{slower} than full precision on AVX-VNNI Intel and $4.8\times$ slower on Apple Silicon. The cause is structural: the iSTFTNet decoder is dominated by transposed convolutions and an inverse-STFT block that the runtime's dynamic-quantization path cannot map to integer kernels, so it wraps still-floating-point operators in quantize/dequantize overhead. Realized and theoretical savings here differ in sign. We also found that whole-model quantization is unusable as a draft even in principle, because it shifts the duration predictor and misaligns the output; only a decoder-only split preserves alignment.

\centering
\caption{Kokoro decoder wall-clock for five utterances, best of three runs after warm-up, single process.}

\section{Toward Allocation-Native Decoding}
\label{sec:roadmap}

We sketch, without claiming results, how the removable pool could become skipped computation. \textbf{(i) Frame-level gating:} decoders that skip or cheaply approximate frames flagged as perceptually slack (pauses, steady vowel interiors), with a cheap draft and an audibility check before accepting it. \textbf{(ii) Band-limited decoding:} generating only the bands whose content exceeds an energy threshold, leaving the rest to a cheap fallback. \textbf{(iii) Training-time awareness:} distilling a student decoder whose capacity is conditioned on a predicted audibility map. Our measurements bound what any such design can gain. The decoder accounts for 63\% (Piper) and 94\% (Kokoro) of kernel time on x86 (Section~\ref{sec:rq2}); if decoder cost fell in proportion to the fraction $f$ of output skipped, the Amdahl ceiling on stage-level speedup would be $1/(1-d f)$ for decoder share $d$; for $f=0.5$ that is about $1.5\times$ (Piper) and $1.9\times$ (Kokoro). This is a ceiling under an assumption we have \emph{not} demonstrated (that cost scales with retained output), not a measured speedup. The same measurements warn against shortcuts: post-hoc dynamic INT8 quantization made the decoder slower on both CPUs we tested (Section~\ref{sec:rq4}).

\section{Discussion}

Our results refine rather than defeat perceptual compute allocation. The \emph{premise}, that a large fraction of generative audio output is perceptually irrelevant and can be identified and removed transparently, is supported by the metric and, preliminarily, by single-listener pilots; a larger listening study would turn this into a quantitative claim. The \emph{principle} (allocate by audibility, not uniformly) beats uniform and random allocation decisively. What our data remove is one piece of the original mechanism story: MP3's psychoacoustic model is not needed to drive the allocation; signal energy is a sufficient and nearly free criterion for time--frequency removal. This is good news for deployment (the ``eye-tracker'' costs nothing) and a caution for novelty claims (the sophisticated model is not the contribution).

The binding constraint is realization. A fixed neural circuit does not become cheaper because its output is ignorable; the savings must be installed, and post-hoc precision reduction does not install them on CPU. The path forward is \emph{allocation-native decoding}: decoders that can decline to synthesize masked frequency regions or perceptually slack frames, which is an architecture and training question rather than an inference-time trick. Our measurements bound the prize: the decoder is 63--96\% of cost, and a large majority of its output is removable.

\section{Related Work}

\textbf{Psychoacoustics in neural audio.} Masking models allocate bits in perceptual codecs~\cite{iso11172, painter2000perceptual, bosi2003introduction}, including neural codecs with psychoacoustically guided multi-band bitrate allocation~\cite{muffin2025}. Zhen et al.\ weight training losses psychoacoustically to ship smaller static models~\cite{zhen2018psychoacoustically, zhen2020calibration}; that is training-time gradient weighting, whereas we allocate inference computation dynamically. \textbf{Adaptive computation.} Early exit, confidence-gated depth, and learned routing are established for classification and language modeling~\cite{teerapittayanon2016branchynet, schuster2022calm, raposo2024mixture, han2022dynamic} and, in speech, for understanding-side models~\cite{daisy2024}. On the synthesis side, recent work applies speculative decoding~\cite{leviathan2023fast, chen2023accelerating} to autoregressive TTS~\cite{ssd2025}, alongside learned dynamic chunk policies~\cite{dcar2025} and scheduled early exit in spoken language models~\cite{spark2026}, with confidence, learned, or fixed criteria, but never an audibility criterion. Static efficient synthesis (LPCNet~\cite{valin2019lpcnet}, FastSpeech~2~\cite{ren2021fastspeech2}) and static step-reduction for flow-matching TTS~\cite{zheng2025epss} spend fixed, content-independent compute. \textbf{Perceptual allocation in vision.} Foveated rendering~\cite{guenter2012foveated, patney2016foveated} and foveated neural reconstruction~\cite{kaplanyan2019deepfovea} allocate rendering budget by visual acuity, and foveated diffusion~\cite{foveateddiffusion2026} extends this to generative image and video synthesis; a VR line applies spatial auditory acuity to sound-propagation rendering~\cite{peng2025foveation}. We position CPU-TTS perceptual compute allocation as an independent audio-side instantiation of the same axis. Every neighbor shares at most two of our three coordinates (dynamic inference-time allocation, an audibility criterion, and a synthesis task) and none shares all three.

\section{Limitations}

Human evidence is limited to two single-listener pilots by the author, who is not naive to the stimuli; it supports direction, not a quantitative transparency claim. A pre-registered multi-listener ABX study and a MUSHRA-style non-inferiority study with trained listeners are future work. PESQ is an imperfect proxy whose severities we show to be unreliable here. Two model families, one language with Hinglish through an English grapheme-to-phoneme model, and Model-1-class PAMs. No end-to-end speed-up was achieved: H2 is unrealized, and we report no capacity multiplier because we have no measured end-to-end reduction to base one on. The 96\,dB full-scale calibration is a conservative codec convention; below-threshold fractions are playback-level dependent.

\section{Conclusion}

Perceptual compute allocation is a sound organizing principle for CPU speech synthesis: the perceptually removable pool is large, audibility-ordered allocation dominates uniform allocation, and, unexpectedly, a trivial energy criterion suffices to find the pool, with the heavyweight psychoacoustic model offering no advantage for time--frequency removal. The open problem is installation: turning removable output into skipped computation demands allocation-native decoders, since post-hoc precision reduction fails on CPU. We release our tooling, corpus, calibration probe, listening-study instrument, and the full harness, including every negative-result experiment, so that the next step is reproducible.

\section{Claim Discipline}
\label{app:discipline}

The following rules were fixed before experimentation and were not relaxed. (1) Capacity multipliers are reported only from measured end-to-end wall-clock reduction $r$ as $1/(1-r)$; stage-level and end-to-end reductions are always distinguished; none is reported here because no end-to-end reduction was measured. (2) Illustrative numbers from planning never appear as results. (3) All overheads (allocation decisions, mechanism switching) live inside every reported efficiency number. (4) No fabricated or interpolated measurements; no synthetic listening-test data. (5) Fixed seeds, pinned tool versions, every claim traceable to a logged run. (6) Inference claims are CPU-only on named hardware; any training-side or quantization compute is disclosed as such.

\section{ABX Study Protocol}
\label{app:abx}

Stimuli are level-normalized 16-bit PCM. Each listener completes 2 practice rounds with feedback, then 30 test rounds (6 per budget, utterances sampled without repeating an utterance--condition pair) interleaved with 6 anchor rounds, all shuffled; assignment of reference and processed clip to A/B and to X is randomized per round. Screening excludes listeners with anchor accuracy below 5/6 or median test-round response time below 1.5\,s. Analysis pools trials per condition and reports accuracy with Wilson 95\% intervals and one-sided exact binomial tests against 0.5. For a future multi-listener study (about 30 screened listeners), the rule fixed in advance is: a budget is declared transparent only if accuracy is not significantly above chance ($p\geq0.05$) \emph{and} the Wilson upper bound is at most 0.60. The pilot in Section~\ref{sec:abx} used this instrument with one listener and is not subject to that rule. Consent, anonymity, and the right to withdraw are stated on the study's first screen.
